FreeBee

A handheld powdered sugar applicator for organic varroa mite treatment in beehives — designed, built, and field-validated at Dartmouth's O-Farm.

The Problem

Beekeepers had no good tools for organic mite treatment.

Varroa destructor is the leading cause of managed honeybee colony collapse worldwide. Left untreated, infestations kill a colony within two years. Treatment options fall into two categories: chemical miticides, which are effective but leave residues in wax and honey, and organic methods — primarily powdered sugar application — which are residue-free but rely on improvised tools or manual dusting that result in inconsistent dosing and poor coverage.

No purpose-built, handheld organic mite applicator existed on the market. Beekeepers improvising with modified hand-cranked dusters or shaker jars couldn't control dosage, struggled with even distribution across frames, and added significant time to an already labor-intensive practice.

The gap wasn't a design problem anyone had solved — it was one nobody had noticed was worth solving. I noticed it by watching beekeepers work.

My Role

From class project to independent development.

FreeBee originated in ENGS 21 — Engineering Problem Solving and Design — at Dartmouth's Thayer School of Engineering. I led the mechanism design and the dispensing system architecture throughout the class, working with a team of four. After the course ended, I continued the project independently, retaining full IP ownership and pursuing grant funding to develop it toward a market-ready product.

I was awarded the Thomas D. Sayles Research Grant ($2,000) from Dartmouth in fall 2026 to continue development — one of a small number of undergraduate research grants awarded each term. The current development push focuses on integrating electronics from the ground up: a motor-driven sifting mechanism, brushless fan system, and a unified housing designed for field durability and ease of cleaning.

Process

Start with the beekeeper. Design backward from there.

The first step wasn't sketching — it was watching. Beekeepers need both hands free during a hive inspection. Treatment takes place between frames with limited clearance. The device needs to be operable with gloves on, easy to reload, and resistant to the humid, propolis-coated environment inside a hive.

Those constraints defined the design brief more precisely than any specification document. From there the process moved through concept sketches, rough foam and cardboard models to test grip and proportion, early 3D-printed prototypes to validate mechanism geometry, and iterative testing to tune dosage rate and coverage.

Materials & Construction

Designed for the field.

Material selection was driven by the operating environment: humid, propolis-coated interior of an active hive, requiring durability, chemical resistance, and field repairability. The current development iteration uses:

PETG

Primary housing — chemical-resistant, food-safe, impact-tolerant

TPU

Vibration-isolation mounts — absorbs motor vibration, maintains seal integrity

PETG-CF

Internal structural brackets — carbon-fiber reinforced for stiffness at reduced weight

PA-CF

High-wear components — nylon-carbon composite for parts under repeated mechanical stress

What's Next

From validated prototype to field-ready product.

The Sayles Research Grant is funding a full electronics integration push in fall 2026. The planned bring-up sequence moves from a basic button/LED circuit through motor control, brushless fan integration via ESC, full power management, and a state machine driving the complete device logic — all within a unified housing designed for ease of field cleaning and repair.

Longer term, I'm exploring a modular snap-together 3D-printable hive frame in multiple sizes for different hive types — designed for distributed self-fabrication by beekeepers as an access and equity design choice, with STL files shared freely so anyone with a printer can fabricate at cost.

FreeBee targets the roughly 3.8 million managed honeybee colonies in the United States (USDA Census of Agriculture), with a primary focus on small-scale and hobbyist beekeepers who represent the majority of operations and who currently have the fewest purpose-built tools available to them.
