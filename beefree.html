FreeBee

A purpose-built handheld applicator that disperses powdered sugar for non-chemical varroa mite treatment in beehives — developed through Dartmouth's Thayer School of Engineering and funded by a Sayles Research Grant.

The Problem

Beekeepers had no purpose-built tool for organic mite treatment.

Varroa destructor is the leading cause of managed honeybee colony collapse worldwide — untreated infestations can kill a colony within two years. Chemical miticides work, but they leave residues in wax and honey and drive mite resistance over time.

The organic alternative — dusting bees with powdered sugar — is residue-free and triggers a grooming response that dislodges mites. But it's held back by application, not chemistry. Manual sifting and shaker jars produce inconsistent dosing and uneven coverage. In interviews, beekeepers told us the real barrier is the absence of a known, repeatable dose — without it, powdered sugar never became a method they could trust and schedule around.

State of the Art

Why not just use an existing duster?

Beekeepers do improvise — modified leaf blowers, hand-crank sifters, and general pest-control powder dusters like the Exacticide. The fact that people adapt these tools confirms the demand. But every one of them is a repurposed device, not a hive-designed one.

Adapted dusters don't meter a dose, so beekeepers can't apply a quantified treatment. They clog as powdered sugar absorbs the humidity inside a hive. And professional pest-control units are priced and built for a different user. FreeBee is designed the other way around: a beekeeper-first device that integrates a motorized de-clumping sifter with an air-delivery system to place a measured, even dose exactly where it's needed.

My Role

From class project to independent, grant-funded development.

FreeBee began in ENGS 21 — Engineering Problem Solving & Design — at Dartmouth's Thayer School of Engineering, where I led the dispensing-mechanism design on a four-person team. We took it from problem definition through a validated physical prototype.

After the course, I continued developing it independently. In fall 2026 I was awarded a $2,000 Thomas D. Sayles Research Grant to build a fully integrated version two — replacing the proof-of-concept's separate parts with purpose-built electronics, rechargeable power, and a unified, field-durable housing.

Process

Nine concepts, a weighted decision, and a lot of iteration.

The design process started with the user. Beekeepers need both hands free, work in tight clearances between frames, and operate with gloves on in a humid, propolis-coated environment — constraints that shaped the brief more precisely than any spec sheet.

We generated nine solution concepts and scored them against seven weighted criteria — efficacy, dosage repeatability, ease of use, non-invasiveness, durability, cost, and safety — which pointed to a modular air-delivery duster. From there the work moved through concept sketches, rapid 3D-printed prototypes, and iterative testing to solve the core problem: getting fine, clump-prone sugar to mix with an airstream and distribute evenly. A fluid analysis of the air path (balancing velocity against nozzle area) tuned the final geometry.

We then tested the prototype on a live colony at Dartmouth's Organic Farm, with hive-owner approval and review by the campus sustainability office. A single treatment raised observed mite drop from 1 to 18, with no observed swarming or colony disruption — the first real-world evidence that the device does what it's meant to.

Materials & Construction

Designed for the field.

Material selection is driven by the operating environment — the humid, propolis-coated interior of an active hive — and by the need for field durability and easy cleaning. The current build uses three printed materials:

What's Next

From validated prototype to field-ready product.

The Sayles grant is funding a full electronics-integration push: a single-button control system (microcontroller, brushless fan via an electronic speed controller, a vibration-driven sifter, rechargeable power, and automatic sequencing) built into one consolidated, field-cleanable housing.

The development plan runs in iteration cycles — design, fabricate on a 3D-printer farm, bench-test against the ENGS 21 benchmarks, then validate on live hives with local beekeepers. The goal by term's end is a consolidated prototype ready for small-batch reproduction, shaped by the people and the bees it's built for.

FreeBee targets the roughly 2.8 million managed honeybee colonies in the United States, with a focus on small-scale and hobbyist beekeepers — the majority of operations, and the ones with the fewest purpose-built tools available to them.
